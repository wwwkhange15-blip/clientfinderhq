How to write a cold email that gets freelance clients to reply

Most cold emails fail for one reason: they talk about the sender. A good one is short, specific to the reader, and ends with a question that is easy to answer.

1. Write to a person, not a company inbox

Emails to info@ or contact@ are rarely read by the person who decides. Look for the owner, founder or marketing manager on the company website or LinkedIn, and address them by name.

2. Keep the subject line plain

Short and specific works best: "Idea for your homepage" or "Quick question about your newsletter". Avoid all-caps, hype and fake "Re:" subjects. They look like spam and damage trust.

3. Use four short lines

Line one is something specific you noticed about their business. Line two is who you are and what you do, in one sentence. Line three is a small piece of proof, such as a sample you made. Line four is a simple question.

Hi Sara, I saw that your pricing page has no testimonials. I write short customer stories for small software companies. Here is a sample I wrote for another brand: [link]. Would it be useful if I drafted one for you?

4. Stay under 100 words

People read cold emails on their phones between other tasks. If it takes more than 20 seconds to read, it is too long. Cut your life story, your list of services and any attachments.

5. Ask for a small next step

"Can we schedule a 30-minute call?" is a big ask from a stranger. "Would you like me to send the sample?" is easy to say yes to.

Be honest and respectful

Write each email by hand to a real business, mention only work you have actually done, and make it easy to say no. Do not buy large email lists. Sending the same message to thousands of people breaks anti-spam rules in many countries and gets your address blocked.

Track what happens

Note who you emailed, the date, and who replied. After 30 emails, look at which subject lines and openings worked, and keep those. Replies are never guaranteed, but a small, steady process improves your odds over time.
